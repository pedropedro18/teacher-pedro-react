Memory, we are inclined to believe, functions as a kind of archive — a faithful repository of experience, retrievable at will, unaltered by the passage of time. This assumption, however comforting, is fundamentally at odds with what contemporary neuroscience has revealed about the reconstructive, rather than reproductive, nature of remembering.

Each act of recollection is not a retrieval of some pristine original but a reassembly, subtly reshaped by present circumstance, emotional state, and the accumulated weight of subsequent experience. We do not so much remember the past as we continually reinvent it, layering interpretation upon interpretation until the original event becomes indistinguishable from the narrative we have constructed around it.

This has profound implications, not merely for our understanding of individual identity — which is, after all, largely constituted through the stories we tell ourselves about who we have been — but for our collective relationship to history itself. If memory is malleable at the level of the individual, how much more so is it at the level of a society, where competing narratives vie for primacy, and where the very act of commemoration is inseparable from the politics of selective forgetting?

Perhaps, then, the question we ought to be asking is not whether our memories are accurate, but what purpose their particular inaccuracies serve. What do we gain, psychologically or socially, from remembering certain events in the manner we do? And what, correspondingly, do we permit ourselves to forget?

To interrogate memory in this way is not to descend into a paralysing relativism, wherein no account of the past holds more validity than another. Rather, it is to acknowledge that remembering is, at its core, an act of meaning-making — one that reveals as much about who we are now as it does about who we once were.
